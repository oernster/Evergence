# Tragic Choices: Necessity Without a Blank Cheque

[← Index](README.md) · [Benevolent strength](BENEVOLENT_STRENGTH.md) · [Evidence and time →](EVIDENCE_AND_TIME.md)

## Problem statement

Some legitimate decisions offer no entirely harmless course. Refusing to decide can itself inflict harm. Conversely, labelling a choice a *necessary evil* can obscure alternatives or excuse conduct that would otherwise be unjustifiable.

**Candidate rule:** where harm cannot be avoided, seek the least harmful **ethically and practically defensible** course after independently challenging the set of assumed options and the claim of necessity.

This is not a formula for aggregating people into expendable units. Certain rights and prohibitions may constrain which options are admissible, even when a decision-maker predicts better overall outcomes from violating them. Those constraints require legitimate, revisable deliberation in the broader [ethical framework](../ETHICS.md).

## Decision record: a proposed minimum protocol

1. **Define the concrete danger.** What happens without intervention? Separate confirmed observations from forecasts and rhetoric.
2. **Expand the option set.** Is the apparent binary choice false? Can delay, negotiation, evacuation, additional resources, limited intervention or consent create another path?
3. **Establish boundaries.** Exclude options that inherently violate applicable rights or constraints; do not conceal such violations behind favourable arithmetic.
4. **Estimate harms with uncertainty.** Whom does each option affect, immediately and over time? Include distributional impacts, reversibility and confidence limits.
5. **Test necessity and proportionality.** Require explicit reasons why less harmful feasible alternatives are inadequate.
6. **Seek independent challenge.** Give affected parties and dissenting reviewers a meaningful voice insofar as circumstances permit.
7. **Authorise narrowly.** Assign responsibility, define permitted action, safeguards, duration and termination criteria.
8. **Publish and correct.** Preserve the decision record; provide review, remedies and lessons without retrospectively rewriting the evidence.

## The affected persons' conception of a good outcome

When identifying harms, a decision-maker must not presume that higher aggregate wealth, output or prestige offsets the loss of basic security or self-directed life for particular people. The proposed [definition of winning](WHAT_COUNTS_AS_WINNING.md) requires listening to affected communities and distinguishing necessary protection from an architect's preferred model of flourishing.

## Limits of optimisation

- **No facile arithmetic:** smaller aggregate harm is not sufficient if the outcome depends on discrimination, dehumanisation, avoidable cruelty or treating innocent people as interchangeable units.
- **No permanent emergency:** urgency may shorten deliberation but must not make special powers perpetual.
- **No success by popularity alone:** a decision can be legitimate while unpopular and popular while unjustified.
- **No immunity through benevolence:** good intentions do not cancel foreseeable consequences.
- **No certainty theatre:** acknowledge the possibility that evidence and forecasts were wrong.

## Illustrative abstract scenario

A protective system faces an escalating risk to two communities. Immediate intervention may prevent serious harm but restrict some freedoms; inaction preserves those freedoms now yet may permit avoidable injury. A legitimate assessment would examine narrower safeguards, preventive communication, independent confirmation of the threat, less intrusive contingency support and a fixed review time. It would not declare an unlimited emergency simply because every option carries cost.

## Falsification and review

A decision procedure that habitually produces hidden harms, excludes affected groups, makes emergency status permanent or treats every dilemma as binary is failing its stated purpose. Such evidence should trigger design revision rather than increased secrecy.
