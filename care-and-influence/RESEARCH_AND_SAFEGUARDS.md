# Care Beyond Reciprocity: Research Programme and Safeguards

[← Care Beyond Reciprocity](README.md) · [Influence and gratitude](INFLUENCE_AND_GRATITUDE.md) · [Research programme](../RESEARCH.md)

## Research purpose

Determine whether civilisational institutions can support voluntary, non-intrusive benevolence and beneficial knowledge transmission while protecting autonomy, privacy, equality and independent judgement.

The proposal's moral goals are normative; claims that particular programmes improve wellbeing, trust, security or resilience must be tested. Outcomes should be disaggregated across affected communities rather than inferred from aggregate approval or publicity.

## Candidate questions

1. Under what conditions do anonymous, mutual-aid or stranger-helping practices reliably benefit recipients rather than increasing vulnerability or pressure to reciprocate?
2. When do public recognition programmes reward genuine contribution and when do they incentivise performance, exaggeration or selective assistance?
3. How might basic rights and dependable public services alter the role of voluntary charity, without extinguishing civic generosity?
4. What protects recipients from a donor who uses help to seek loyalty, personal access, reputational leverage or silence?
5. Which educational or cultural interventions help people distinguish evidence-based appreciation of a public contributor from assumptions of personal acquaintance or endorsement?
6. How can archival practice preserve uncertain provenance and meaningful intellectual lineage while respecting privacy and avoiding fabricated attribution?
7. How might benefits of kindness and knowledge transfer cross generations and what uncertainties limit causal attribution?

## Proposed evaluation protocol

- Define the recipient's independently expressed interests and available alternatives before declaring an outcome beneficial.
- Identify all affected groups, including people who supply unpaid care or who are excluded from assistance.
- Distinguish expressed intentions, observed outcomes, reported experience and downstream conjecture.
- Consider consent, dignity, effects on individual autonomy, durability of benefits, fairness and alternatives to intervention.
- Invite adverse findings, dissent and independent review; document failures rather than suppressing them.
- Avoid intrusive measurement: no compulsory behavioural tracking, emotional-scoring or public naming of recipients.
- Record selection effects: apparent successes may be concentrated among those easiest to assist or most willing to be observed.

## Threat model: what could go wrong?

| Failure mode | Candidate guardrail |
| --- | --- |
| **Kindness becomes leverage** | Leverage works only on gratitude that is felt; no expectation of access, obedience, publicity or repayment absent a separate voluntary agreement; no manufacturing of gratitude by any institution. Where a family's formation of a child fails, the surrounding systems carry the duty of care as an entitlement, which creates no debt in the child. |
| **Uninvited help becomes paternalism** | Preference for consent, reversible offers and recipient-defined needs; narrowly constrained exceptions for urgent danger. |
| **Public charity masks systemic neglect** | Maintain enforceable basic entitlements and inspect unmet needs, not only donated resources. |
| **Recognition becomes a leaderboard** | Avoid ranking human moral worth by visible good deeds or turning benevolence into a compulsory performance metric. |
| **One-way admiration is mistaken for mutual relationship** | Clearly distinguish mediated influence from personal contact and reciprocal consent. |
| **Unknown provenance becomes invented certainty** | Preserve documentary uncertainty; never infer a benefactor without evidence. |
| **Anonymous help conceals wrongdoing** | Allow proportionate, independently reviewable financial and safeguarding checks without blanket exposure of recipients. |
| **Care work becomes limitless obligation** | Safeguard caregivers' freedom, health, time, compensation where due and the right to decline unreasonable demands. |
| **Data collection chills generosity** | Minimise personally identifiable information; retain only what a legitimate public purpose requires. |

## Institutional decision test (proposed)

Before introducing a programme explicitly justified as nurturing care, ask:

1. **Who asked for it and who can decline?**
2. **What independently verifiable need or opportunity does it address?**
3. **What guaranteed right or existing duty might be displaced?**
4. **Who gains power over whom, including through gratitude or dependency?**
5. **What privacy, attribution and independent-redress safeguards apply?**
6. **What evidence would show the programme has caused more harm than benefit?**
7. **Can those affected challenge, revise or end it without retaliation?**

## Hypotheses, not guarantees

- Freely offered care *may* improve social trust when recipients retain autonomy and can refuse assistance.
- Recognition of overlooked maintenance and mentoring *may* improve provision but rewards can distort motivation and attention.
- Teaching distinctions between influence, evidence, gratitude and actual relationship *may* protect autonomy, though effects require testing.
- Historical preservation *may* facilitate later beneficial discovery but cannot guarantee either discovery or wise application.

No metric can establish the whole moral worth of a person or relationship. Institutional assessments should remain plural, contestable and proportionate to their purposes.

## Adoption boundary

This proposal cannot justify compulsory affection, mandatory gratitude, forced altruism, intrusive social scoring, surveillance of private relationships or restricting access to basic necessities based on a person's displayed kindness. Revisit institutional failures through the oversight and fallibility principles already present elsewhere in Evergence; do not create an exceptional authority to decide who genuinely cares.
