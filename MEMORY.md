# Civilisational Memory: Preserve, Contextualise, Learn

[← README](README.md) · [Architects](ARCHITECTS.md) · [Mathematics →](MATHEMATICS.md)

## Core principle

**Do not deliberately erase authentic evidence of past errors merely because it is shameful, dangerous, inconvenient or politically unwelcome.** Civilisations that lose their history lose opportunities to recognise recurring failure.

Records of successes, suffering, atrocities, scientific mistakes, institutional failures and dissenting accounts should survive transitions wherever physically possible.

Preservation does not mean every detail must be universally broadcast.

## Three separate duties

1. **Preservation:** retain authentic, verifiable source material and provenance.
2. **Access:** provide legitimate, reviewable paths to retrieve historical material.
3. **Interpretation:** supply evidence-based context and warnings while keeping original records distinct from commentary.

Restricted access to particularly harmful operational details may be warranted; restrictions then require stated reasons, proportionate scope, independent oversight and periodic reconsideration.

The aspiration that all civilisational life can obtain archived material must be reconciled with privacy, protection of victims and risks from instructions that enable serious harm. Access policies should be challengeable rather than absolute.

## Authenticity and historical disagreement

Preserve original versions, provenance, corrections and competing interpretations. Do not silently replace records with sanitised narratives. An architect's explanation is not the historical record itself.

A civilisation's account of itself is not self-certifying. Independent archivists and provenance checks held outside the institution being recorded are the practical form of that caution (speculation, by analogy with incompleteness; see [MATHEMATICS.md](MATHEMATICS.md) section 9).

Recommended archival practices include:

- Redundant, geographically and institutionally distributed copies.
- Cryptographic integrity checks and documented provenance.
- Open, migratable formats and periodic readability tests.
- Versioned annotations rather than destructive edits.
- Independent archivists and appeals against access restrictions.
- Privacy-aware retention, minimisation of unnecessary personal data and protection for victims.
- Long-term disaster recovery and continuity planning.

## Mathematical sketch

Let Hₙ denote the historical record available at civilisational stage n. The ideal of non-destructive preservation is:

\[
H_n \hookrightarrow H_{n+1}
\]

where the arrow denotes a faithful, retrievable embedding of prior records into the next stage's archive.

This is an **ideal requirement**, not a physical guarantee. Storage media fail; information has thermodynamic costs. A real design needs explicit reliability assumptions and recovery strategies.

Those costs make preservation an allocation decision. [ECONOMICS.md](ECONOMICS.md) gives memory a floor, \(M_n\ge M_n^{\min}\), in a budget it shares with survival and evolution; a complete archive kept forever is not available on a finite budget, so the three duties above are costed choices rather than free ones. The floor is a duty, not a cost to be minimised.

## Memory should inform, not imprison

History is evidence for future decisions, not a command to reproduce past norms. Future generations must be able to reinterpret evidence and reject inherited assumptions while retaining access to the underlying sources. The same holds for authority: a community may change the root of its legitimate authority while the record of the earlier root, the transition and the dissent stays recoverable ([plural rooting](plural-rooting/README.md)).
