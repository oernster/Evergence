# Distributing Protective Capability, Not Reproductive Outcomes

[← Adjunct index](README.md) · [Rights](RIGHTS_AND_REPRODUCTION.md) · [Governance](GOVERNANCE_AND_RESEARCH.md)

## Define the problem correctly

'Key individuals' can mean unique specialists, trusted mediators, educators, engineers, medical workers, emergency responders, accountable governors or people offering unusual insights. The objective is **not** to rank human worth or allocate particular persons. The measurable question is whether communities can access the *functions* required to remain safe and flourish.

A civilisation should plan for sudden absence, departure, disability, disagreement, conflict, exhaustion and institutional failure. Any design dependent upon a uniquely 'correct' human being is brittle.

## Six concrete mechanisms

1. **Capability redundancy:** train more than one person for critical functions; enable succession without worshipping irreplaceable individuals.
2. **Portable knowledge:** well-maintained, comprehensible procedures and open, preserved archives; avoid single-holder trade secrets for indispensable civic knowledge, while respecting privacy and intellectual property rights.
3. **Voluntary mutual-aid networks:** locally governed, cross-regional response teams and exchange programmes providing expertise where needs are greatest without compelled relocation.
4. **Baseline universal opportunity:** equitable education, healthcare, nutrition, safe housing, accessible communications and mentoring so that abilities are developed rather than assumed to be predetermined at birth.
5. **Distributed institutions:** independent oversight, multiple care providers, emergency surge capacity and community-reachable legal remedies so no single leader or faction monopolises protection.
6. **Community-led need assessment:** ask residents where services fail; prioritise documented shortfalls without drawing conclusions about the genetic or moral qualities of residents.

## Diagnosing shortfalls without ranking people

For a region `r` and civic function `f`, analysts might estimate **service coverage** `C(r,f)` and a locally negotiated **minimum viable service level** `M(r,f)`. The indicative service gap is:

```text
G(r,f) = max(0, M(r,f) - C(r,f))
```

This is *not* an objective to equalise individual skills, births or populations. The measure is incomplete: it can miss cultural context, informal care, practical access and the wishes of communities. Report uncertainty, review the threshold, protect personal data and avoid automatically assigning people or triggering sanctions based on the number.

## Response ladder

**Ordinary service gap:** invest in facilities, training and local access; offer voluntary opportunities to contribute or relocate.

**Acute capability gap:** time-limited external assistance, remote expert consultation, emergency resource sharing and clear succession planning, with local participation and published oversight.

**Credible present-day threat:** assess verifiable conduct and circumstances through a lawful, multidisciplinary process focused on prevention and safeguarding; responses should be specific, proportionate, reviewable and based on what is actually occurring.

**Unsupported claims about destiny or past lives:** do not label a child or prospective parent dangerous; do not reorganise reproduction or custody around an unverified premise. Preserve relevant claims as claims, not as verified evidence of identity.

## Failure and abuse tests

- A 'talent registry' becomes a caste system or compulsory migration programme.
- A numerical coverage target pressures families to produce children with selected traits.
- Risk models exclude minority or disabled people or downgrade ordinary caregiving.
- Expertise concentrates in elites even as aggregate numbers appear healthy.
- 'Emergency necessity' suspends consent and due process indefinitely.

Any of these outcomes requires correction or termination of the implicated policy, not a stronger enforcement mechanism.
