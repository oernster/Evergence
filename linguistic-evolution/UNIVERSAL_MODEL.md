# Universal Model: Language as a Culturally Evolving System

[← Extension index](README.md) · [Archival stewardship →](ARCHIVAL_STEWARDSHIP.md)

## Scope and epistemic status

**Empirical basis.** Languages change under cultural transmission, learning, use and contact. Iterated-learning research provides formal and experimental ways to investigate such change (Kirby, Griffiths & Smith, 2014; [Sources](SOURCES.md)).

**Hypothesis.** Some expressions, categories and rhetorical patterns may replicate successfully in part because they compress assumptions, offer convenient labels or help coordinate institutions, including in ways that obscure moral consequences.

**Analogy, not established fact.** Describing a language as a *memeplex*, evolving quasi-lifeform or information organism illustrates selection, variation and inheritance. It does **not** demonstrate autonomous agency, a genome, a single fitness function or a biological species. The replicating units themselves may be difficult to isolate.

## Units of study

Analyse language at separable scales:

1. **Token or phrase:** a euphemism, metaphor, category, slogan, abbreviation or term of art.
2. **Discourse pattern:** repeated framing, passive constructions that hide agents, 'us versus them' labelling, bureaucratic depersonalisation or dehumanising narratives.
3. **Institutional practice:** the way authorities, media, organisations, education and laws adopt or enforce terminology.
4. **Language ecology:** transmission between speakers, multilingual contact, translation, historical shifts and oral, visual or signed modalities.
5. **Social outcomes:** measurable effects on comprehension, moral judgement, policy acceptance, social exclusion, coercion or violence, with the distinction between association and causation maintained.

An entire language is an **ecology of heterogeneous practices**, not a single moral actor. No simple score can honestly encode its overall ethical value.

## Candidate mechanism: compression and propagation

```text
Underlying proposition or assumption
    ↓  lexicalisation, metaphor, shorthand, bureaucratic euphemism
Compact expression or reusable discourse pattern
    ↓  teaching, copying, repetition, prestige, incentives
Familiarity and institutional uptake
    ↓  possible reduced scrutiny, diffusion of responsibility
Behavioural effects in a particular political and social context
    ↺  resulting institutions may reinforce the expression
```

Every arrow is a **testable possibility**, not a guaranteed causal link. Some shorthand improves clarity and accountability; familiarity can sometimes increase criticism; and the same expression can have benign, malign and changing meanings. Power, law, material incentives, intimidation, prior prejudice and organisational structures may explain outcomes better than linguistic design.

## Generalisation across every language

The **unit of comparison is a contextual mechanism**, not a supposedly defective language. A complete study must accommodate:

- Different writing systems and languages without standard orthographies.
- Spoken, signed, visual, oral and digitally mediated communication.
- Polysemy, dialects, code-switching, translation and changing meanings.
- Language used for resistance, parody, solidarity and protection as counterexamples.
- Culture-specific concepts that do not translate into an exact equivalent.
- Institutions that export harmful terminology across languages and borders.

For instance, 'collateral damage' in English and *Sonderbehandlung* in Nazi administration can both prompt investigation of how language may distance a decision-maker from harmed people; they are **not equivalent terms, events, magnitudes or proven mechanisms**. Their meaning and use must be evaluated historically and separately.

## A deliberately modest formalisation

Let an observed case be a tuple:

```text
Q = (expression, time, community, context, institutions, audience, outcome)
```

A proposed explanatory model is:

```text
P(outcome | expression, context, institutions, history)
```

The object of study is the **difference between credible counterfactual conditions**, not a raw correlation between words and harm. No universal probability is asserted here. The model is incomplete until outcomes, comparison groups, selection criteria and confounding variables are operationally defined.

Possible observations include contextual comprehension tests, archive records of when terms entered policy, longitudinal discourse evidence, translation comparisons and documented institutional decision processes. Association never by itself establishes that a language caused an atrocity.

## What would challenge the theory?

- Harmful policies emerge in the absence of the hypothesised expressions or patterns.
- The same expressions are commonplace in non-harmful settings without any supporting mechanism.
- Institutional pressure explains both language and conduct, with no detectable incremental influence from the wording.
- A predicted effect disappears under matched controls, preregistration or replication.
- Ordinary clarification and education outperform any stronger proposed intervention.
- A feature proposed as harmful turns out to be shared by languages with no comparable history, which is the test a politeness convention such as *bitte* answering *danke* fails (see the [German case study](GERMAN_CASE_STUDY.md)).

These would not invalidate cultural evolution in general; they would constrain or refute the **specific linguistic-risk hypothesis**.

## Intervention hierarchy (least intrusive first)

Evidence-appropriate responses may include: contextual explanation; media and historical literacy; voluntary alternative terminology; transparent wording in public administration; institutional revision of euphemistic policies; independent auditing of persuasive systems; and preserving historical originals and all dissenting interpretations. Decisions affecting public speech must still be grounded in law, fundamental rights and independent review.

The question for Evergence is therefore **whether a replicating linguistic mechanism in a concrete context produces preventable harm**, not whether a language deserves to exist.
